REFERENCE - SCORES

ASVAB Scores Explained

How AFQT percentiles, standard scores, and Army line scores work, and how to raise yours

Your AFQT Percentile

Armed Forces Qualification Test (AFQT)

Your AFQT score is a percentile from 1 to 99, not a percent correct. A score of 65 means you scored better than 65 percent of a nationally representative reference group, not that you answered 65 percent of questions correctly.

It's calculated from just three inputs: Arithmetic Reasoning (AR), Mathematics Knowledge (MK), and a Verbal Expression (VE) composite built from Word Knowledge (WK) and Paragraph Comprehension (PC), with the verbal composite counted twice in the total.

Standard Scores

Before your subtest results turn into an AFQT or line score, each subtest is converted to a standard score: a scale with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10. A standard score of 50 means average performance against the reference group, and because the scale follows a roughly normal distribution, a standard score of 60 or higher puts you in about the top 16 percent of test takers on that subtest.

Standard scores are what feed into the AFQT formula above and the Army line score composites below, so raising a single subtest's standard score can move more than one number on your final report.

What Is a Good Score?

There's no single "good" AFQT score. What counts as good depends on what you're trying to do:

  • To enlist: your real target is your branch's minimum. Anything at or above it clears the eligibility bar.
  • To have your pick of jobs: a score in the 50-64 range is solid and opens most military occupational specialties; 65 or higher is strong, placing you in Category II or above.
  • For bonuses and special programs: some enlistment bonuses and competitive specialties set their own higher AFQT or line score floors, so a higher score generally helps even once you're past the minimum.

Know your goal first, then work backward to the score that actually matters for it.

Army Line Scores

Beyond the AFQT, your subtest standard scores combine into line scores, composites reported on a scale with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 20 that determine which specific jobs (MOS) you qualify for.

The Army's General Technical (GT) score, for example, combines your verbal composite and Arithmetic Reasoning standard scores. Other line scores, like Combat (CO), Electronics (EL), and Mechanical Maintenance (MM), draw on different combinations of subtests. Every MOS sets its own minimum line score requirements, so two applicants with the same AFQT can still qualify for very different jobs depending on their subtest performance.

Mission ASVAB estimates all 10 Army line scores on your results page after a Full Army Assessment. These are a documented practice estimate, not official scoring. Only your official CAT-ASVAB administered at MEPS or a Military Entrance Test (MET) site counts for enlistment.

How the CAT-ASVAB Decides Your Questions

Most testing sites now use the CAT-ASVAB, a computer adaptive test. It doesn't hand every test taker the same fixed set of questions. Instead, it picks each question based on how you answered the one before it: answer correctly, and the next question gets harder; answer incorrectly, and it gets easier.

That also changes how your score is calculated. The scoring model weighs the difficulty of the questions you answered, not just your raw count correct, so two test takers who answer the same number of questions correctly can end up with different scores if one of them worked through harder material.

Because it's adaptive, you also can't go back and change an earlier answer once you've moved on. That's why both pacing and accuracy matter: rushing into wrong answers early can lock you into an easier, lower-scoring path, while spending too long on any one question eats into time you need later in the section.

How to Raise Your Score

Start by finding out where you actually stand. Take the Starting-Point Diagnostic, a 20-minute test that scores your AR, WK, PC, and MK performance and tells you which sections need the most work, before you commit to a full-length practice test.

From there, drill your weakest areas specifically rather than reviewing everything evenly. Brush up on math formulas, work through the word list for Word Knowledge, and follow a study plan to stay organized as your test date approaches.

Finally, practice under timed conditions. Since the CAT-ASVAB adapts to your accuracy question by question, practicing at real test pace, not just untimed review, builds the speed and consistency that carry over to test day.
