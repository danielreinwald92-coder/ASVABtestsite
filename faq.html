ASVAB Frequently Asked Questions

Straight answers about scores, eligibility, difficulty, and how to prepare

What is the ASVAB?

The Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery (ASVAB) is a multiple-choice test used by every branch of the U.S. military, including the Army, Navy, Air Force, Marine Corps, and Coast Guard, to determine whether you qualify to enlist and to match you with military jobs that fit your strengths.

It measures aptitude across subjects like math, verbal skills, general science, and mechanical and electronics knowledge, then uses those results for two purposes: setting your enlistment eligibility (the AFQT score) and identifying which specific military occupational specialties (MOS) you qualify for (line scores).

What is the AFQT score?

The Armed Forces Qualification Test (AFQT) score is a percentile from 1 to 99 that compares your performance to a reference group of test takers. It is calculated from only four of the ASVAB subtests: Arithmetic Reasoning (AR), Word Knowledge (WK), Paragraph Comprehension (PC), and Mathematics Knowledge (MK).

Your AFQT score is what determines whether you are eligible to enlist at all. The other ASVAB subtests do not affect AFQT eligibility, but they do affect which specific jobs you qualify for.

What ASVAB score do I need to enlist?

Minimum AFQT scores are commonly cited as 31 for the Army, 31 for the Navy, 32 for the Marine Corps, 36 for the Air Force, and 40 for the Coast Guard.

Applicants who score between 21 and 30 may still qualify for the Army through its Future Soldier Preparatory Course, an academic program designed to help candidates raise their AFQT score to meet the standard minimum.

These minimums shift with each branch's recruiting needs, so confirm the current numbers with a recruiter before you test.

What is a good ASVAB score?

A score of 50 or higher means you outperformed half of the reference group and opens up most military jobs. A score of 65 or higher places you in Category II, and 93 or higher places you in Category I, the top tier.

What counts as a good score really depends on the job you want. Some competitive military occupational specialties require higher AFQT or line scores than others, so your target matters as much as the raw number.

Is the ASVAB hard?

The subject matter covers material most test takers saw in high school: algebra, reading comprehension, vocabulary, and general science, so nothing on the test is exotic.

What makes it feel hard is that the CAT-ASVAB is adaptive: answer correctly and the next question gets harder, so strong performers see a tougher test than their raw score alone might suggest.

In practice, targeted preparation matters more than raw difficulty. Reviewing your weak areas before test day makes a bigger difference than the test's inherent hardness.

How long is the ASVAB?

The CAT-ASVAB (the computerized version used at most testing sites) typically takes about 2 to 3 hours to complete.

Because it is adaptive, computing your ability level from your answers and skipping questions that would not add information, most people finish faster than they would on the older paper-and-pencil version, which uses a longer, fixed set of questions for every test taker.

Can you use a calculator?

No. Calculators are not allowed on any version of the ASVAB, computerized or paper.

You will be given scratch paper for calculations, so it is worth practicing arithmetic and math problems by hand before test day.

How many times can you take the ASVAB?

You can retake the ASVAB one month after your first attempt. If you want to test again after that second attempt, you will need to wait another month for the next retake, and then at least 6 months between any further retakes.

Scores are generally valid for 2 years.

Retake rules can change, so confirm the current policy with a recruiter before you plan around it.

What are line scores?

Line scores are composites built from combinations of your ASVAB subtest scores. They determine which specific military jobs you qualify for, separate from the AFQT score that determines basic enlistment eligibility.

The Army, for example, uses line scores like General Technical (GT), Clerical (CL), and Combat (CO), each combining different subtests. Every job (MOS) has its own minimum line score requirements, so two applicants with the same AFQT can still qualify for very different jobs depending on their subtest performance.

What is the difference between the CAT-ASVAB and the paper ASVAB?

The CAT-ASVAB (computer adaptive test) selects each question based on how you answered the previous one, so it needs fewer questions to estimate your ability, and you cannot go back to change earlier answers.

The paper ASVAB is a fixed-form test: every test taker answers the same set of questions in the same order, and you can review and change answers within a section before moving on.

Most testing today happens on the computerized CAT-ASVAB.

Do I have to take Assembling Objects?

The official CAT-ASVAB includes an Assembling Objects (AO) subtest, which measures spatial reasoning.

Mission ASVAB does not include AO in its practice tests, because AO is not used to calculate your AFQT score or any of the Army line scores this site covers. Skipping it lets your study time go toward the sections that actually move your eligibility and job qualification numbers.

Do practice test scores predict my real score?

A well-built practice test can give you a directionally useful estimate of where you stand, but it is not a guarantee of your official score.

Mission ASVAB documents its scoring model openly, so you know exactly what is an estimate and what is official government math. See how AFQT and line scores are calculated for the details.

Only your official test at MEPS or a Military Entrance Test (MET) site counts for enlistment.

How should I start studying?

Start with the 20-minute Starting-Point Diagnostic. It covers all four AFQT subjects and tells you which sections need the most work, without the time commitment of a full-length test.

From there, review your weak areas and follow a structured study plan to build up your skills before attempting a full practice test.
